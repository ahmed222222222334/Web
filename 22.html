The era of the emergence of Islam and the Rightly-Guided Caliphs

Before the advent of Islam in the seventh century, there was a continuous intermingling of Christians in Palestine with the Arab population (many of whom were also Christians) living to the south and east of Palestine. Muslims turned to Jerusalem as the direction of prayer, and the miracle of the Isra and Mi'raj, which recounts the Prophet Muhammad's journey from Mecca to Jerusalem and his ascension to the seven heavens, according to Islamic tradition, also conferred sacred significance on the city. After the Battle of Ajnadayn and the Muslim victory, Muslims began to control the lands of Palestine. The Arab Muslims captured Jerusalem from the Byzantines in 637 AD. Caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab expressed his respect for the city by accepting its surrender. He wrote them a document of security that later became known as the Pact of Umar. He described the conquest and the document as unique for that era, as the document provided security for their lives, their money, their churches, and their crosses. Whoever left it was safe, and whoever stayed was safe. This was witnessed by Khalid ibn al-Walid, Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, Amr ibn al-As, and Muawiyah ibn Abi Sufyan. The Arabic name given to Jerusalem was Bayt al-Muqaddas, corresponding to the Sacred House. The Byzantine province of Palestine became an Arab administrative and military province, called Jund Filastin (the Soldiers of Palestine) from that time on.

Umayyad era

Muawiyah ibn Abi Sufyan was the first Umayyad caliph and governor of Damascus. When the Umayyads seized power, they moved the capital of the caliphate to their capital, Damascus. The Umayyad Caliphate reached the height of its power, extending its influence to Central Asia in the east and the borders of France in Europe in the west, making it the largest Islamic empire in history. The fifth Umayyad caliph, Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, ordered the construction of the Dome of the Rock between 688 and 692 AD on top of the Rock of Ascension. It remains an architectural symbol of the city to this day. Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan allocated seven years of Egyptian tax revenue for this construction, but Abd al-Malik died before the mosque was completed. His son, al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik, completed it. Some see the Umayyads' preference for Palestine and Jerusalem as being political in some sense, as Mecca was in the hands of the Umayyads' opponents in the early decades. However, even after Mecca and Medina pledged allegiance to the Umayyads in 692 AD, the seventh Caliph, Suleiman, was installed in Jerusalem and later in the capital, Damascus. He also completely rebuilt the city of Ramla, which had previously not existed.

Abbasid era

The Abbasids seized the caliphate from the Umayyads after the Battle of the Zab. They moved the caliphate's capital to Kufa, and built Baghdad, making it their capital. The Abbasid Empire reached the height of its power and influence within a century of its establishment. After that, much of the empire's territory fell under the rule of its rulers, whose loyalty to the Abbasid Caliphate was nominal. For most of the period from the end of the ninth century until the Crusades at the end of the eleventh century, Palestine remained ruled by Muslim rulers based in Cairo. Two Abbasid caliphs visited Jerusalem. The first was Al-Mansur, the second Abbasid caliph. He visited Jerusalem twice and ordered the repair of damage caused by an earthquake. The second caliph was Al-Mahdi, the third Abbasid caliph. He visited Jerusalem specifically to perform prayers at Al-Aqsa Mosque and expanded the perimeter of Al-Aqsa Mosque, which led to rapid urbanization and civilization. Al-Ma'mun, the seventh Abbasid caliph, ordered major renovations to the Dome of the Rock, under the supervision of his brother. Harun al-Rashid issued a decree permitting the Byzantine Roman Emperor Charlemagne to restore Jerusalem's churches and allowed him to send builders and funds to build their churches. He then issued a decree providing protection for every Christian wishing to visit the Christian holy sites in Jerusalem. Muslim soldiers protected Christian visitors from any harm until they completed their visit.

The time of the Crusader Wars and the campaigns against them

The Arab and Islamic rule of Palestine was interrupted by the Frankish invasion. Several Crusader states arose along the eastern Mediterranean, including the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem in Palestine between 1099 and 1187 AD. However, the anti-Frankish campaigns led by Saladin and his successors continued until 1291, when the Muslims recaptured the last Frankish strongholds in Caesarea and Acre. After entering Jerusalem, the Franks tortured, burned, and slaughtered thousands of unarmed Muslim men, women, and children, in addition to the small number of Jewish residents who took refuge in their synagogue. Several wars took place during this period, in which Muslim leaders tried to liberate Palestine, including the attempt of the Fatimid vizier al-Afdal, which failed.

Mamluk era

The Mamluks expelled the last Crusaders from Palestine. They defeated the Mongols under Hulagu Khan, the grandson of Genghis Khan. Their rule lasted from 1260 until the Ottoman rule of Egypt in 1517. It remained known as "Jund Filastin" and was divided into six districts (Gaza, Lydda, Qaqun, Jerusalem, Hebron, and Nablus). Despite the Muslim victory at the Battle of Ain Jalut and the decline of the Mongol invasion, the Crusaders retained the Kingdom of Acre, which controlled the coastal region extending from Jaffa to Acre. After that, the Mamluk sultans undertook to purge the remaining Crusaders from the Levant, even 30 years after the Battle of Ain Jalut. Al-Zahir Baybars succeeded Sultan Qutuz, and continued to purge the Levant of the Franks, making concessions and concessions as necessary. After Baybars resolved the internal conflicts, he headed to Acre to wage war against the Crusaders in 662 AH (1263 AD). Upon his arrival, the Crusaders requested a renewal of the truce, but he refused and decided to attack to determine their strengths and weaknesses. Al-Zahir Baybars set out for Palestine again in 1265 AD, attacking Caesarea and capturing it. The following year, he set out for Safed, besieging and conquering it. He set out for Palestine again in 1267 AD and asked the Crusaders for peace. He made concessions to some but not to others so as not to consolidate their forces. In 1268 AD, he succeeded in liberating Antioch. Sultan Al-Mansur Sayf al-Din Qalawun continued the liberation campaign, forming a tripartite alliance between the Crusaders, the Tatars, and the rebellious deputy governor of Damascus, but their alliance failed. After that, Al-Mansur tightened the noose on the Crusaders, occupying the fortress of Marqab in 1285 AD and Latakia in 1289 AD. The Sultan took advantage of the incident of the Franks killing a number of Muslim merchants and declared jihad and began preparing for it, but he fell ill and died suddenly in 1290 AD, and was succeeded by his son Al-Ashraf. The Franks offered Al-Ashraf peace, but he refused and continued his preparations. He marched to Acre and, with the help of the Syrian army, was able to liberate it in 1291 AD. After that, he captured Sidon, Tyre, Haifa and Atlit. Thus, the Frankish presence in Palestine and the Levant ended after two centuries (1096-1291 AD).

Ottoman rule

The Ottomans defeated the Mamluks in 1517, and the Ottoman Empire took control of Palestine in 1516, after the Battle of Marj Dabiq on August 23 of that year. Sanjak is called the Sanjak of Jerusalem, Gaza, Safed, Nablus and Lajjun, all of which belong to the state of Damascus, but the rule was to a large extent in the hands of the local population. The reconstruction of public utilities in Jerusalem was by Suleiman the Magnificent in 1537. The various parts of Palestine and the whole of the Levant fell under the control of families and multiple entities during the Ottoman Empire period, ranging from loyalty to the central state. On November 2, 1831, Muhammad Ali Pasha, the rebellious Ottoman governor, sent his son Ibrahim Pasha in a campaign against the state of Syria, which has been under Alawite rule for ten years (until February 19, 1841. It was a revolution in May 1834). The rule of Abraham was suppressed within months. The great powers did not recognize the conquests of Muhammad Ali and his son Ibrahim, and was later forced to evacuate from the parties he conquered. In 1869, the Ottoman Empire issued a decision to allow land ownership of foreigners, and in general 1875 The first secret association against Ottoman rule arose in Beirut, then similar associations spread throughout the state of Syria.
