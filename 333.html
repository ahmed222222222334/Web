Zionist occupation

The Palestinian issue, or the Palestinian-Israeli conflict, refers to the political and historical dispute and humanitarian problem in Palestine, dating from 1897 (the First Zionist Congress) to the present day. It is an integral part of the Arab-Israeli conflict and the resulting crises and wars in the Middle East. This conflict is intrinsically linked to the emergence of Zionism, Jewish immigration to and settlement in Palestine, and the role of the major powers in regional events. The Palestinian issue also revolves around the issue of Palestinian refugees, the legitimacy of the State of Israel, and its occupation of Palestinian territories through several stages. This has resulted in massacres committed against Palestinians, resistance operations against the Jewish state, and numerous United Nations resolutions, some of which have been historic, such as Resolutions 194 and 242. This conflict is considered by many analysts and politicians to be the central issue in the Arab-Israeli conflict and the cause of the crisis and tension in this region. Although this conflict occurs within a relatively small geographic area, it attracts significant political and media attention due to the involvement of numerous international parties. The world's major powers are often involved, given its location in a sensitive region and its connection to problematic issues that constitute the culmination of contemporary world crises. These issues include the conflict between East and West; the relationship between Judaism, Christianity, and Islam; Arab relations with the West and the importance of Arab oil to Western countries; the importance and sensitivity of the Jewish issue within Western civilization, especially after World War II and the Holocaust; and issues of anti-Semitism and the pressure exerted by Jewish lobbies in the West. On the Arab level, many Arab thinkers, theorists, and even politicians consider the Palestinian-Israeli conflict to be the central issue and crisis in the region. Some thinkers often link it to the Arab Renaissance, totalitarian regimes, and the weakness of democracy in the Arab world.

Pre-British Mandate period

Some believe that the ambitions of modern Western Jews in the Palestinian territories began in 1561 AD, when the Portuguese Jew Joseph Nasi, who was considered the richest man in the world at the time, tried to build a colony for Western Jews to flee the persecution they were subjected to in the West.

The emergence of the Zionist movement and resistance

In the 1880s, Western Jews began adopting new theories for colonizing Palestinian lands based on the idea of ​​replacing attempts at civil or peaceful control with armed control. One of the most prominent proponents of this theory was the World Zionist movement, which declared: "The day we establish a single Jewish regiment is the day our state will be established." In the mid-1880s, the Zionist movement in Europe formed the "Lovers of Zion" group (the First Zionist Congress was held in Basel in 1897). This movement called for the establishment of a state for Jews, and many Zionists believed that this state should be located in the historical Jewish state, the area known as Palestine. Palestine was then part of the Ottoman Empire and had a local government (a vilayet), and the area was populated primarily by Arab Palestinians (Jews remained less than 8% until 1920). This Zionist project was met with widespread popular anger throughout Palestine and categorically rejected by all political figures at the time, including the Mufti of Jerusalem, Amin al-Husseini, Izz ad-Din al-Qassam, and later Abd al-Qadir al-Husseini, as well as other political, religious, and military leaders. This marked the beginnings of the emergence of popular resistance in Palestine. Arab figures and rulers differed in their approach to this project. Some supported the Palestinians in achieving their destiny, while others remained silent. Some reached out to leaders of the Zionist movement to gain the approval of the British government, such as Prince Faisal bin Hussein, who met with Chaim Weizmann, head of the World Zionist Organization, and others. As for Western countries, they welcomed the Zionist project in Palestine, and it received financial, military, and logistical support from major powers such as Britain, the United States, and France. These powers viewed the Hebrew state that the Zionists aspired to establish in Palestine as a protection of their interests in the region.

Great Arab Revolt

Sharif Hussein bin Ali declared a revolution against the Turks in the name of all Arabs. The principles of the Arab Revolt were agreed upon by Hussein and the leaders of the Arab associations in the Levant and Iraq in an Arab National Pact, the goal of which was to achieve Arab independence and establish a strong, united Arab state. Palestine was among the regions comprising this future state. The British government promised the Arabs, through the Hussein-McMahon Correspondence (1915), to recognize Arab independence in exchange for their participation in the war alongside the Allies against the Turks. Al-Qibla newspaper published an official statement raising the four-colored Arab flag starting from Sha'ban 9, 1335 (June 10, 1917), the first anniversary of the revolt. However, Britain broke its promise to the Arabs, ratifying the Sykes-Picot Agreement and subsequently the Balfour Declaration, to consolidate the Zionist presence in Palestine and separate the country from its Arab surroundings.

Sykes-Picot Agreement

In 1916, a secret agreement was concluded between France and Britain, with the approval of the Russian Empire, to divide the northern part of the Arab lands (Iraq and the Levant) between France and Britain. This agreement determined the spheres of influence in the Arab Levant after the collapse of the Ottoman Empire, which had controlled this region, following its defeat in World War I. It was decided that the area later cut off from southern Syria and known as Palestine would be under international administration (except for the Negev Desert), to be agreed upon in consultation between Britain, France, and Russia. However, the agreement stipulated that Britain would grant the ports of Haifa and Acre to France, with France having free use of the port of Haifa in exchange for Britain's free use of the Syrian port of Alexandretta, which was under French control. Later, to alleviate the embarrassment suffered by the French and British after the revelation of this agreement and the Balfour Declaration, Churchill's White Paper was issued in 1922, clarifying in a more lenient tone the objectives of British control over Palestine. However, the content of the Sykes-Picot Agreement was reaffirmed at the San Remo Conference in 1920. Subsequently, the League of Nations Council approved the mandate documents for the territories concerned on June 24, 1922

Balfour Declaration

Since the beginning of the twentieth century, England adopted a policy of creating a political Jewish entity in Palestine. They believed that this entity would remain subject to their influence, dependent on their protection and care, and would in the future be a source of distraction for the Arabs, exhausting their strength and causing them constant anxiety, hindering any attempt at unity. Britain crowned this policy with the Balfour Declaration, issued by its then Foreign Secretary. There were shared interests with a strategic dimension. Essentially, Britain was concerned about the immigration of persecuted Jews from Russia and Eastern Europe. It found it in its interest to utilize this process in its expansion program in the Middle East. Following the declaration, it diverted convoys of immigrants to Palestine and provided them with protection and the necessary assistance. British Foreign Secretary Arthur James Balfour then issued a declaration, on behalf of the British King, to the leaders of the Zionist movement on November 2, 1917, promising the establishment of a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. This declaration was opposed by the Arabs, who were deceived by Britain when it promised them independence if the Arabs stood by it against the Ottomans.

Faisal-Weizmann Agreement

On January 3, 1919, the Faisal-Weizmann Agreement was signed by Prince Faisal, son of Sharif Hussein, with Chaim Weizmann, head of the World Zionist Organization, at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919. It provided the Jews with facilities to establish a homeland in Palestine and recognized the Balfour Declaration.

British Mandate period

In 1917, the British army took control of Palestine and Transjordan with the help of the Arab Revolt led by Sharif Hussein (who sought independence and unity for the Arab states based on the Hussein-McMahon Correspondence). The Sykes-Picot Agreement was implemented, and Jordan and Palestine became a British mandate. In the same year, Arthur James Balfour, the British Foreign Secretary, sent a letter to Baron Lionel Walter de Rothschild, pledging Britain's support for the establishment of a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine, while noting that this would not prejudice the civil and religious rights of non-Jews in Palestine. This later became known as the Balfour Declaration.

Jewish immigration

According to official statistics, 367,845 people (Jews and non-Jews) immigrated to Palestine since the end of the nineteenth century, of whom 33,304 immigrated legally between 1920 and 1945. Between 50,000 and 60,000 Jews, and a small number of non-Jews, immigrated illegally during this period. Immigration accounted for most of the increase in the Jewish population, while the increase in non-Jews was largely due to natural population growth. There is no reliable data on immigration to Palestine from Arab countries. Britain initially treated both Arab and Jewish populations with caution, but under the pretext of anti-Semitism in Europe, which had grown during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Jewish immigration (mostly from Europe) to Palestine began to increase significantly, creating considerable Arab resentment. This led the British government to impose restrictions on Jewish immigration to Palestine, issuing a White Paper to halt and regulate Jewish immigration to Palestine. These quotas were controversial, particularly in the final years of British rule. A growing sentiment developed in many Arab countries to fight the British and some Jewish organizations, which attacked Arab populations in retaliation for attacks on Jewish communities. The Jews relied militarily on the Haganah, a semi-secret militia that cooperated with the British authorities during World War II and later fought the British and Arabs on the eve of the abolition of the Mandate. More extreme Jewish organizations, such as the Irgun and the Stern Gang (Lehi), were also active during this period, carrying out terrorist operations and waging a violent campaign against Arab and British targets.

The Great Palestinian Revolution

It is one of the largest popular revolutions carried out by the Palestinian people against the British colonists and Jewish immigrants to Palestine during the British Mandate over Palestine, such as the 1920 Revolution, the 1921 riots, the 1929 Buraq Revolution, and the 1933 riots. It lasted for three consecutive years, starting from 1936 to 1939, following the death of Sheikh Izz al-Din al-Qassam at the hands of the British police in Jenin. A general strike was then declared, encompassing most of the Palestinian Arab cities.

Resolution to partition Palestine

This is the name given to a resolution adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on November 29, 1947, which stipulated the termination of the British Mandate over Palestine and the partition of its territory into three new entities: the establishment of an Arab state and a Jewish state on Palestinian soil, and the placement of the cities of Jerusalem and Bethlehem in a special zone under international trusteeship. This resolution, officially known as General Assembly Resolution 181, was one of the first attempts to resolve the Arab/Jewish-Zionist conflict over the land of Palestine. The idea of ​​partitioning Palestine into two states, Arab and Jewish, with an international zone around Jerusalem, emerged in the Peel Commission Report of 1937 and the Woodhead Commission Report of 1938. These reports were issued by two committees appointed by the British government to study the Palestine issue following the Great Palestinian Revolt of 1933–1939. After World War II and the establishment of the United Nations to replace the League of Nations, the United Nations called for a review of the mandates granted by the League of Nations to the European empires, and considered the case of the British Mandate over Palestine to be one of the most complex and important issues.

Political life before 1948

Political awareness in Palestine began early, and this awareness was noticeable during the Ottoman Empire. Palestine played a significant role in the Ottoman Empire, as the people of Palestine had representatives in the Chamber of Deputies, which was elected following the promulgation of the constitution. Ruhi al-Khalidi, Sa'id al-Husayni, and Hafez al-Sa'id were representatives of the Jerusalem District, Sheikh Ahmad al-Khammash of the Nablus District, and As'ad al-Shuqairi of the Acre District. Palestinian intellectuals played a significant role in confronting the policy of Turkification and confronting Jewish immigration to their country. They established approximately 17 organizations and political parties to express their views and defend their national rights, many of which were linked to regional and Arab issues. Among the most important of these parties and organizations were: the Muslim-Christian Associations, the Arab Independence Party, the Free Palestinian Party, the Palestinian Communist Party (National Liberation League), the Muslim Brotherhood, the Palestinian Farmers' Party, the National Coalition Party, the Arab Youth Congress, and the Palestinian Arab Party.

The Nakba

During the British Mandate of Palestine, and even after the establishment of the State of Palestine, the Zionist movement carried out a series of pre-planned actions aimed at displacing Palestinians and ethnically cleansing Palestine. These actions included targeting Palestinian villages and cities with terrorist attacks by the Haganah, Irgun, and Stern Gang. These pre-planned tactics have been discussed by several historians, including Ilan Pappe, Benny Morris, and Walid Khalidi. These operations led to the Jewish seizure of approximately 78% of the area of ​​historic Palestine, and the killing and forced displacement of between 750,000 and one million Palestinians to neighboring countries and other parts of Palestine. The Palestinian refugees who left the territories on which Israel was established formed a new nucleus for the Palestinian cause. Between 1947 and the 1948 war, approximately 750,000 Palestinian Arabs were displaced from their towns. After the end of the war, the mandate area was divided between Israel, Jordan, and Egypt. Israel granted Israeli citizenship only to those who remained within its borders and refused to allow the return of displaced Arabs from outside these borders. Jordan granted citizenship to the residents of the West Bank, including its refugees. As for the residents of the Gaza Strip and its refugees, they remained without citizenship, as Egypt refused to grant them Egyptian citizenship. Today, refugees constitute nearly half of the Palestinian population, approximately 6.4 million people (2020).

Deir Yassin massacre

Deir Yassin is a Palestinian village located west of Jerusalem. A horrific massacre took place on April 9, 1948, at the hands of the two Zionist gangs, the Irgun and Stern Gang. This occurred two weeks after the signing of a peace treaty requested by the heads of the neighboring Jewish settlements and accepted by the people of Deir Yassin. Large numbers of the village's residents, including children, the elderly, women, and young people, were killed in this massacre. The exact number of victims is disputed. Arab and Palestinian sources state that between 250 and 360 were killed, while Western sources state that the number did not exceed 107. The Deir Yassin massacre was a significant factor in the Palestinian migration to other parts of Palestine and neighboring Arab countries due to the terror it caused among civilians. The massacre further intensified the already existing hatred between Arabs and Israelis. The establishment of the State of Israel

The establishment of the State of occupation

After the end of World War II in 1945, Zionist gang attacks on British forces in Palestine escalated, prompting Britain to refer the Palestinian issue to the United Nations. On April 28, the UN General Assembly session on the Palestine issue began, concluding on May 15, 1947, with the decision to establish the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP), an 11-member committee. This committee published its report on September 8, with most members supporting the partition solution, while the remaining members recommended a federal solution. The Arab Higher Committee rejected the partition proposal, while the Jewish Agency announced its acceptance of the partition. The United States and the Soviet Union agreed to the partition, respectively. On October 29, the British government announced its intention to leave Palestine within six months if a solution acceptable to the Arabs and Zionists was not reached. In the period that followed, the pace of military operations escalated on all sides. The Zionists had carefully planned and implemented plans, controlling every area from which the British forces withdrew. Meanwhile, the Arabs were in a state of military crisis due to the delay in taking effective measures to build a regular Arab force to defend Palestine. The Zionist forces succeeded in occupying areas larger than what they had obtained under the partition resolution, and large numbers of Palestinians left their cities and villages due to the fighting or out of fear of the massacres they had heard about. On May 13, Chaim Weizmann sent a letter to US President Truman asking him to fulfill his promise to recognize a Jewish state. He announced the establishment of the State of Israel in Tel Aviv on May 14 at 4:00 PM. The British High Commissioner left his official headquarters in Jerusalem for Britain. Within minutes of May 15, the British Mandate over Palestine ended, and the declaration of the establishment of the State of Israel became effective. The United States recognized the State of Israel ten minutes later, but the fighting continued, and this was now a war between the State of Israel and the neighboring Arab states. By the end of the war, Israel had become a reality, controlling areas exceeding those stipulated in the partition plan. It occupied the entire coastal plain of Palestine (as divided by the British Mandate), with the exception of the Gaza Strip, which was controlled by the Egyptians. It also occupied the entire Negev, Galilee, and northern Palestine. East Jerusalem and the West Bank became part of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan. A broader front of conflict with the Arab states began

1948 War

The first war the Arabs waged after the birth of the modern Arab state was the 1948 war, which the Arabs considered a "catastrophe," calling it the Nakba War. They also call it the Palestine War, while the Israelis call it the "War of Independence." In the Western media, it is referred to as the "First Arab-Israeli War." It erupted following the declaration of the establishment of the State of Israel on Palestinian land on May 15, 1948. Forces from six Arab countries (Egypt, Syria, Jordan, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, and Iraq) entered Palestine to prevent the establishment of a Jewish state there. Military operations continued until January 1949, after Israel had effectively taken control of the areas granted to it by UN Partition Resolution 194 and beyond. At that time, the refugee issue arose with the exodus of more than 400,000 Palestinians from their homes to the West Bank (later annexed by Jordan) and the Gaza Strip (also annexed by Egypt), in addition to neighboring countries and the diaspora. This marked the beginning of the Arab-Israeli conflict. Zionist leaders had begun preparing detailed military plans since early 1945 in anticipation of the upcoming confrontation. In May 1946, the Haganah drafted a plan later called the May 1946 Plan. The general policy of this plan called for "countermeasures." The Arab armies in Palestine achieved significant victories after their entry into Palestine on May 15, 1948. Egyptian forces achieved tangible successes in the southern sector, as did Jordanian and Iraqi forces on the Jerusalem front and the northern West Bank. These operations caused embarrassment for the Zionist forces, which was quickly alleviated by the Security Council's resolution on May 22, 1948, calling for a 36-hour ceasefire. The Arab states rejected this resolution at the time, and the United States and Britain exerted intense pressure, accompanied by threats, on Arab governments. The British delegation to the Security Council submitted a new request for a four-week ceasefire and to control the flow of volunteers and weapons into Palestine during this period. On June 2, the Arab states informed the Security Council of their approval of the resolution, and the fighting effectively ceased on June 11, a period known as the First Truce. However, the wavering will of the Arab rulers at the time, the lack of coordination among the Arab armies despite their sacrifices, the support and training the Zionist gangs had received from Britain since World War II, and the Israelis' superiority in numbers all led to the defeat of the Arab armies and the fall of more than 78% of the land of Palestine to the Jewish state—more than the area allocated to it in the 1947 partition plan, which granted the Jews 55% of the land of Palestine.

All-Palestine Government

It was a government formed in Gaza on September 23, 1948, during the 1948 war, headed by Ahmed Hilmi Abdel Baqi. Jamal al-Husseini toured the Arab world to present the government's declaration to all Arab and Islamic countries and the Arab League. This government received support from some Arab governments and opposition from others, with Jordan, Egypt, and Iraq opposing its establishment. The idea of ​​forming the All-Palestine Government arose when Britain announced its intention to abandon its mandate over Palestine and referred the issue to the United Nations. The Palestinian leadership, represented at the time by the Arab Higher Committee for Palestine, led by Haj Amin al-Husseini, realized the importance of preparing for this event and preempting it by creating a constitutional framework to fill the vacuum that would result from the end of the British mandate. This framework was the establishment of a Palestinian Arab government. The Arab League's support for the government began to crumble, as the League subsequently refused to invite the government to League Council meetings. The Egyptian government also refused to allow the government to operate in the Gaza Strip.

1949 Armistice

After the end of the 1948 war, the Rhodes Accords were signed, imposing an armistice between Israel and Egypt, Syria, Jordan, and Lebanon. Each country signed the agreement separately, except for Iraq. These agreements established the Green Line, which was officially designated as the ceasefire line but effectively became the border between the then-modern State of Israel and neighboring Arab states. Within the Green Line, that is, within Israel, a number of Palestinian Arab towns and cities, as well as mixed cities inhabited by Jews and Arabs, remained. The western part of Jerusalem also remained within the Green Line, as the Green Line passed through the city center. The de facto drawing of the Green Line divided Palestine into three parts: Israel (the largest part, constituting 78% of the area of ​​Palestine), the West Bank (later annexed to Jordan), and the Gaza Strip (annexed to Egypt). The latter two constitute 22% of the area of ​​historic Palestine, and were later occupied by Israel in 1967.

West Bank and Gaza Strip

These two terms began to emerge after the 1948 war, when the State of Israel was established on the lands promised to the Jewish state in the Palestine Partition Plan and on additional lands seized by the Israeli army or transferred to Israel under the Rhodes Agreement. The remaining territories were divided into two non-contiguous parts. Jordan annexed the larger of the two, the West Bank, based on the agreement concluded at the Jericho Conference in 1949, where Palestinian leaders from the West Bank met and demanded unification with Jordan. Parliamentary elections were held, while Egypt imposed military rule on the smaller of the two, the Gaza Strip. In 1956, the Israeli army occupied the Gaza Strip for five months as part of military operations related to the Suez Crisis, then returned it to Egyptian military rule. In the 1967 war, the Israeli army occupied the West Bank and the Gaza Strip, and Israel imposed military rule over them, with the exception of the eastern part of Jerusalem and its suburbs, which Israel annexed to its territory. Thanks to the secret relations between Israel and Jordan, relations between Jordan and the West Bank continued until King Hussein bin Talal of Jordan announced the disengagement decision in 1988, relinquishing the West Bank and severing Jordan's ties with it. In 1982, Israel completed its withdrawal from the Sinai Peninsula under the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty, but the Gaza Strip remained under Israeli military rule. Since its establishment in 1994, the Palestinian Authority has been negotiating the establishment of an independent Palestinian state in the West Bank and Gaza Strip (which together constitute 22% of the area of ​​historic Palestine). Major Palestinian cities such as East Jerusalem, Gaza, Ramallah, Nablus, Hebron, and Jenin are located in these two areas. The PA uses Ramallah and Gaza as temporary headquarters for its institutions, pending negotiations for a solution. Currently, the West Bank and Gaza Strip are governed by a mixed system of governance. While certain parts of them enjoy autonomous rule, other parts remain under Israeli occupation. The political status of the Gaza Strip has been complicated, particularly since the Israeli military withdrew from it in 2005 without an agreement between Israel and the Palestinian Authority on the nature of its authority, and also because Hamas took control of the Strip despite the Palestinian Authority's opposition. Internationally, there is an implicit consensus that these territories will eventually belong to the Palestinian state, and that Israel is the occupying power. There is no international recognition of the annexation of the eastern part of Jerusalem to Israel, but most countries (with the exception of Arab states) consider Jerusalem a place of special importance that should be subject to a special settlement. At the foundation of the international position on these two areas is UN Resolution 242, adopted in November 1967, which states: "Recognizing the principles of a just and lasting peace in the Middle East, the Security Council, Expressing its continuing concern at the grave situation in the Middle East, Emphasizing the inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war and the need to work for a just and lasting peace in which every State in the area can live in security, and Emphasizing also that all Member States, by accepting the Charter of the United Nations, have undertaken to act in accordance with Article 2 of the Charter." The Palestinian and Israeli sides agreed to adopt Resolution 242 as the basis for a settlement between them within the Oslo Accords. The West Bank geographically includes the Nablus Mountains, the Jerusalem Mountains (including the eastern part of Jerusalem), the Hebron Mountains, and the western Jordan Valley. The Jordanian authorities called it the West Bank because it lies west of the Jordan River, while most of the territory of the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan lies east of the river. The Gaza Strip is a narrow strip extending south of the Palestinian coast on the Mediterranean Sea. It has the highest population density in the world due to the large numbers of Palestinians who fled there after the Nakba, and to rapid natural growth, which exceeds 3% annually.

1956 War

Known in the Arab world as the "Tripartite Aggression," in Western media as the "Suez Crisis," and in Israeli media as the "Sinai War," the war took place in Egypt and the Gaza Strip in 1956. France, Israel, and Britain were the aggressors following Gamal Abdel Nasser's nationalization of the Suez Canal. This war is also known as the 1956 War. Israel's occupation of the Gaza Strip lasted several months, extending into 1957.

1967 War

Known to Western and Israeli media as the "Six-Day War," it occurred in 1967 between Israel and Egypt, Syria, and Jordan, with logistical assistance from several Arab countries. It ended with Israel's victory and its seizure of the rest of Palestine (the Gaza Strip and the West Bank), as well as the Egyptian Sinai and the Syrian Golan Heights. Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser temporarily stepped down from power, and more Palestinians were displaced to neighboring countries. The war also inflicted a psychological defeat on the Arab armies, who lost much of their confidence in their military capabilities and combat effectiveness. Meanwhile, the morale of the Israeli army rose, and its slogan "It is the invincible army" became popular. Israel unilaterally announced its annexation of East Jerusalem (which had been administratively under Jordanian control since 1951) immediately after the defeat. The Israeli Knesset resolution, passed on June 27, 1967, authorized the Israeli government to annex the eastern part of Jerusalem, making the entire city the unified capital of the Jewish state. This reinforced Israel's ongoing efforts to Judaize it. Israel immediately began plundering much of the West Bank's resources, particularly its water resources, and systematically Judaizing East Jerusalem. By seizing the West Bank, it was able to improve its strategic position and military maneuverability, eliminating the potential threat posed by the presence of any organized, armed Arab army in the West Bank, the geographical heart of historic Palestine. The war, dubbed the "Naksa" (setback) to avoid being described as a defeat, has become one of the defining moments in Arab history. Despite the passage of more than fifty years, the event still casts a heavy shadow over Arabs, particularly Palestinians, in terms of its story, its consequences, and the way they view the future after it. The repercussions of the 1967 war, or the Naksa, had a profound impact on the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO), which was still young at the time. It resulted in the establishment of new splinter factions whose ideology was closer to Marxism than to Arab nationalism. This was a result of the deterioration of the Arab nationalist project in the post-Naksa period. Palestinian guerrillas began to spread to the surrounding countries, especially Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria. Resistance activity began to emerge outside Palestine after the fall of the West Bank and Gaza Strip to Israel, completing its occupation of the Palestinian territories. It is worth noting that the mass displacement of Palestinians after the 1967 Naksa to neighboring countries, especially Jordan, which already had a large Palestinian population since the 1948 Nakba. Due to its geographical proximity to Palestine, sharing the longest land border with it, this led to the PLO's primary presence in Jordan, a position it maintained until 1971
